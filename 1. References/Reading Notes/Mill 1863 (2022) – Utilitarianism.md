# Utilitarianism

## Abstract

Mill defends the principle of utility against its critics: actions are right as they tend to promote happiness. He distinguishes higher from lower pleasures, answers the usual objections, offers a "proof" of the principle and shows that justice is part of utility, not opposed to it.

## Thesis

The standard of right and wrong is the general [[Happiness|happiness]], pleasure and the absence of pain, for everyone affected ([[Utilitarianism]]).

## Arguments

- **The principle of utility** (II): right actions promote happiness, understood as pleasure and the absence of pain.
- **Higher and lower pleasures** (II): settled by those who know both.
- **Impartiality** (II): my happiness counts no more than anyone else's.
- **Motive and act** (II): the motive judges the agent, not the action; the rules of morality are inherited experience.
- **The proof** (IV): happiness is desirable because people desire it.
- **[[Justice]]** (V): the most important part of utility.

## Counterarguments

- **[[G. E. Moore]]:** going from "desired" to "desirable" is a fallacy.
- **Composition:** from each person wanting their own happiness, it doesn't follow that anyone wants the general happiness.
- **Competent judges:** the test may only reflect the tastes of an educated class.
- **[[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]:** moral worth depends on acting from [[Duty|duty]], not only on consequences.

## Conclusions

The classic statement of [[Consequentialism]]; with [[Jeremy Bentham|Bentham]]'s version, the base of modern utilitarian ethics.

The name comes from [[Jeremy Bentham|Bentham]]; Mill's book defends and corrects his theory. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 12]

#connection he fails, almost grotesquely, to show that there would be any contradiction [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 12] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=16&annotation=730R|PDF]]

Mill against [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]: the categorical imperative only works by looking at the consequences of a rule. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 12]

#claim Questions of ultimate ends are not amenable to direct proof. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 12] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=16&annotation=732R|PDF]]

So the 'proof' in Chapter IV can only be indirect. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 12]

### Chapter II: What utilitarianism is

#definition The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 15] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=19&annotation=734R|PDF]]

The principle of utility: an action is judged by its consequences for [[Happiness|happiness]]. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 15]

#definition By happiness is intended pleasure, and the absence of pain; by unhappiness, pain, and the privation of pleasure. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 15] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=19&annotation=736R|PDF]]

#word grovelling [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 16] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=20&annotation=738R|PDF]]

#question Of two pleasures, if there be one to which all or almost all who have experience of both give a decided preference, irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation to prefer it, that is the more desirable pleasure. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 17] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=21&annotation=740R|PDF]]

Who counts as a 'competent judge'? The test may simply favor the tastes of educated people. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 17]

#agree It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 19] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=23&annotation=742R|PDF]]

Quality, not only quantity, of pleasure: the point where Mill parts from Bentham. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 19]

#claim is not the agent’s own happiness, but that of all concerned [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 26] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=30&annotation=744R|PDF]]

#agree utilitarianism requires him to be as strictly impartial as a disinterested and benevolent spectator [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 26] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=30&annotation=746R|PDF]]

Against the charge that utilitarianism is selfish: my happiness counts no more than anyone else's. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 26]

#claim the motive has nothing to do with the morality of the action, though much with the worth of the agent [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 28] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=32&annotation=748R|PDF]]

Kant would answer that moral worth lies precisely in acting from [[Duty|duty]]. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 28]

#agree There is no difficulty in proving any ethical standard whatever to work ill, if we suppose universal idiocy to be conjoined with it [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 35] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=39&annotation=750R|PDF]]

Reply to 'there is no time to calculate': the rules of morality are humanity's experience of consequences. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 35]

### Chapter IV: Of what sort of proof the principle of utility is susceptible

#disagree the sole evidence it is possible to produce that anything is desirable, is that people do actually desire it. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 48] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=52&annotation=752R|PDF]]

'Visible' means can be seen, but 'desirable' means ought to be desired. [[G. E. Moore]] later called this step a fallacy. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 48]

#disagree each person’s happiness is a good to that person, and the general happiness, therefore, a good to the aggregate of all persons [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 49] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=53&annotation=754R|PDF]]

A fallacy of composition? That each person wants their own happiness doesn't show that anyone wants the general happiness. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 49]

### Chapter V: On the connexion between justice and utility

#definition Justice is a name for certain classes of moral rules, which concern the essentials of human well-being [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 76] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=80&annotation=756R|PDF]]

[[Justice]] as the most important part of utility, not something opposed to it. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 76]

#fact ‘everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one,’ [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 79] [[Mill 1863 (2022) – Utilitarianism.pdf#page=83&annotation=758R|PDF]]

Bentham's dictum, the egalitarian core of the principle. [@mill_utilitarianism_2022, p. 79]
