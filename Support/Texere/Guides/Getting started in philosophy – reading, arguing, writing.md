# Getting started in philosophy

This guide condenses what twelve guides to philosophical writing, a handout on thesis statements and an annotated sample paper agree on: what makes a text good or bad, what changes when it is philosophy, and the words that mark each move. It holds for a seminar essay as much as for an article, a master's thesis or a PhD. The scale changes; the logic doesn't. Each source is cited where it is used and listed at the end.

> [!abstract] In five lines
> 1. Answer **one precise question** with **one claim**, stated early, in one sentence.
> 2. Give **reasons**: premises an opponent would grant, steps a reader can follow.
> 3. Present others **accurately and charitably**, and only as much as your argument needs.
> 4. Raise the **strongest objection** and answer it.
> 5. Write **plainly**: short sentences, one word per concept, every sentence doing work.

---

## 1. Two kinds of good writing

Any good text **communicates**. It says up front what it claims, and it is organized, brief, specific and plain [@huemer_guide_nd]. Its thesis is a full, literal sentence: not a fragment, a question or a metaphor [@american_university_academic_support_center_writing_lab_thesis_2009].

A philosophical text does all that and one thing more: it **argues**. It is a reasoned defence of a claim, written for a reader who doesn't accept it yet. It is not a report of your opinions or of other people's views [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @horban_writing_1993; @rippon_brief_2008]. The method: state the question exactly, then answer it in explicit, modest steps [@rippon_brief_2008; @roberts_7_nd]. Summary and background serve the argument; they are never the point [@jones_how_nd; @millgram_writing_2024].

## 2. Good and bad, at a glance

| | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|
| **Thesis** | One sentence, explicit and contestable, in the first paragraph, saying how you will argue [@tooley_writing_nd; @cartaya_how_nd; @jones_how_nd] | A question, a fragment or a metaphor in place of a claim; held back for the end, or never stated [@american_university_academic_support_center_writing_lab_thesis_2009; @blattner_tips_nd] |
| **Scope** | A small point argued well; one or two strong arguments, in depth [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @horban_writing_1993] | A grand question settled in a few pages; every argument you can think of [@roberts_7_nd; @horban_writing_1993; @rippon_brief_2008] |
| **Argument** | Premises and conclusion explicit; premises a reasonable opponent accepts; each step follows [@jones_how_nd; @roberts_7_nd; @huemer_guide_nd] | Assertions, feelings, "I believe"; premises only someone who already agrees would grant, which begs the question [@jones_how_nd; @horban_writing_1993] |
| **Others' views** | Reconstructed in your own words, in their strongest form, with the reasons behind them; only what your argument needs [@cartaya_how_nd; @rippon_brief_2008; @millgram_writing_2024] | A summary with occasional comments; a straw man; "X believes Y" without the why; quotations instead of explanation [@anon_how_nd; @rippon_brief_2008; @jones_how_nd] |
| **Objections** | The strongest one, in a full paragraph, then your reply, also in full [@blattner_tips_nd; @tooley_writing_nd] | None, or an easy one; objections listed and dropped after a line [@blattner_tips_nd; @rippon_brief_2008; @jones_how_nd] |
| **Structure** | A roadmap, then signposts; one argument per paragraph; a view explained before it is evaluated [@cartaya_how_nd; @tooley_writing_nd; @mendelovici_sample_2010] | Paragraphs that don't serve the thesis; your view and the author's mixed together [@millgram_writing_2024; @cartaya_how_nd; @pryor_guidelines_2012] |
| **Evidence** | Arguments, examples, thought experiments; data only with its relevance explained [@rippon_brief_2008; @huemer_guide_nd] | Authority and dictionary definitions [@rippon_brief_2008]; the story of how you came to your view [@blattner_tips_nd] |
| **Language** | Plain, short sentences; one word per concept; technical words in their technical sense [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @huemer_guide_nd] | Inflated prose, unexplained jargon, synonyms for variety, "begs the question" meaning "raises it" [@jones_how_nd; @millgram_writing_2024; @cartaya_how_nd] |
| **Tone** | Claims measured to the argument; disagreement explained [@huemer_guide_nd; @cartaya_how_nd] | "Obviously", "clearly absurd", polemics; or a hedge on every sentence [@huemer_guide_nd; @cartaya_how_nd; @horban_writing_1993] |
| **Opening and end** | Straight to the point; the end says what was shown, what it costs the opposing view and what is still open [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @mendelovici_sample_2010; @cartaya_how_nd] | "Since the dawn of time…", a dictionary definition, a long warm-up about how important the topic is [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @horban_writing_1993; @rippon_brief_2008] |

## 3. What changes in philosophy

Some habits from other courses don't carry over: philosophy asks for a precision closer to the sciences than to the rest of the humanities, and clarity comes before elegance [@yablo_guidelines_nd].

| | In general writing | In philosophy |
|---|---|---|
| **"I"** | Keep "I think" out of the thesis: it weakens it [@american_university_academic_support_center_writing_lab_thesis_2009] | "I will argue that…" is welcome. What is rejected is opinion in place of argument: "I feel", "in my opinion" [@rippon_brief_2008; @jones_how_nd] |
| **Repetition** | Vary your words | The same word for the same concept; a new word signals a new thing [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @cartaya_how_nd] |
| **Evidence** | Sources, data, experts | Arguments; authority and dictionaries settle nothing [@rippon_brief_2008] |
| **Precise words** | The everyday sense | Valid, sound, true, prove, refute, infer, beg the question: each has a technical sense [@huemer_guide_nd; @rippon_brief_2008] |
| **Questions** | A rhetorical question to draw the reader in | State the question exactly, then answer it; a rhetorical question is not an argument [@rippon_brief_2008; @jones_how_nd] |
| **Quotations** | Support for claims | Few, short, each explained in your own words [@rippon_brief_2008; @pryor_guidelines_2012] |
| **Shape** | Introduction, body, conclusion | Plus a roadmap, the reconstruction of a view, objections and replies [@rippon_brief_2008; @mendelovici_sample_2010] |
| **Ambition** | Coverage | A modest point, well defended; a limit you state openly is a strength [@cartaya_how_nd; @pryor_guidelines_2012] |

## 4. The shape of a good text, from essay to PhD

The skeleton is the same at every size [@rippon_brief_2008; @roberts_7_nd; @mendelovici_sample_2010]:

1. **Title**: plain and informative [@mendelovici_sample_2010].
2. **Introduction**, short: the question, the thesis, why it matters, the roadmap [@anon_how_nd; @rippon_brief_2008; @millgram_writing_2024].
3. **Background**: only what the argument needs, at most a third of the text [@tooley_writing_nd; @millgram_writing_2024].
4. **Reconstruction** of the view you discuss: premises and conclusion explicit, as plausible as you can make them [@cartaya_how_nd; @mendelovici_sample_2010].
5. **Your argument**: exactly where you disagree, or what you add, with examples [@mendelovici_sample_2010; @huemer_guide_nd].
6. **Objections and replies**: the strongest objection, then your answer; one developed fully is worth more than several in passing [@blattner_tips_nd; @rippon_brief_2008].
7. **Conclusion**: what you showed and what remains open [@cartaya_how_nd; @mendelovici_sample_2010].

**Scaling up.** An article adds the debate it enters and what it contributes to it. A master's thesis or a PhD repeats the skeleton in every chapter, each with its own question, thesis and roadmap, and the literature is discussed around claims, not author by author: an orderly argument, not a summary with occasional comments [@anon_how_nd]. Empirical research has its own shape (research questions, method, corpus, results): see [[Metodologia cercetării - Structura unei lucrări științifice]] and [[Metodologia cercetării - Literature Review]]. For the formal rules of a bachelor's or master's thesis at the Faculty of Philosophy (University of Bucharest), see [[Ghid de redactare – Licență și disertație (Facultatea de Filosofie, Universitatea din București)]].

## 5. Keywords: the words that mark each move

| Move | English | Romanian |
|---|---|---|
| **Thesis** | I argue that, I will argue, my thesis is, I will show that | susțin că, voi argumenta, teza mea este, voi arăta că |
| **Roadmap, signposts** | First, I will…; in section 2; I now turn to; having shown; to conclude | mai întâi voi…; în secțiunea 2; trec acum la; după ce am arătat; pentru a încheia |
| **Reason** | because, since, given that, after all, for one thing | pentru că, deoarece, întrucât, de vreme ce, dat fiind că, datorită |
| **Conclusion** | therefore, thus, hence, so, it follows that, this means that, we can conclude | prin urmare, așadar, deci, rezultă că, de aceea, iată de ce, aceasta înseamnă că |
| **Contrast, turning point** | however, but, yet, on the contrary, by contrast, rather than, unlike | însă, dar, totuși, or, dimpotrivă, în schimb, spre deosebire de |
| **Concession** | although, though, admittedly, even if, notwithstanding | deși, cu toate că, chiar dacă, în pofida |
| **Objection** | one might object, it could be argued, critics would say | s-ar putea obiecta, s-ar putea spune că, criticii ar spune |
| **Reply** | I reply, in response, even if we grant, this objection fails because | răspunsul meu, ca răspuns, chiar dacă admitem, obiecția nu reușește pentru că |
| **Example** | for example, for instance, take the case of, another example | de exemplu, de pildă, să luăm cazul, un exemplu |
| **Thought experiment** | suppose, imagine, a counterexample | să presupunem, să ne imaginăm, un contraexemplu |
| **Analogy** | similarly, likewise, just as, in the same way, the same applies to | la fel ca, în mod similar, tot astfel, în același mod |
| **Definition, distinction** | by X I mean, I will call, in the sense of, the distinction between, necessary and sufficient condition | prin X înțeleg, voi numi, în sensul de, distincția dintre, condiție necesară și suficientă |
| **Precise words** | valid (arguments), sound, true or false (statements), prove and refute (only for success), infer (people) vs imply (statements), beg the question (circularity) | valid, solid, adevărat sau fals, a dovedi și a respinge, a infera vs a implica, petitio principii |

The signposts follow [@jones_how_nd; @cartaya_how_nd; @rippon_brief_2008; @roberts_7_nd]; the precise words, [@huemer_guide_nd; @rippon_brief_2008].

**Red flags**: words that weaken a text.

| | English | Romanian | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opinion instead of argument** | I feel, in my opinion, personally | simt că, după părerea mea, eu personal | an opinion is not a reason [@jones_how_nd; @american_university_academic_support_center_writing_lab_thesis_2009] |
| **Overclaiming** | obviously, clearly, surely, undoubtedly, proves | evident, este clar că, cu siguranță, fără îndoială, dovedește | claim only what the argument shows [@huemer_guide_nd; @cartaya_how_nd] |
| **Grand opening** | since the dawn of time, throughout history, the dictionary defines | dintotdeauna, de-a lungul istoriei, conform dicționarului | begin with your point [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @rippon_brief_2008; @blattner_tips_nd] |
| **Dismissive words** | ridiculous, clearly absurd, no one could believe | ridicol, evident absurd, nimeni n-ar putea crede | answer the strongest version [@cartaya_how_nd; @huemer_guide_nd] |
| **Stock phrases** | it is important to note, delve, shed light on, basically, "some argue" with no one named | este important de menționat, trebuie menționat că, joacă un rol crucial, „unii susțin” fără niciun nume | words that do no work [@millgram_writing_2024; @huemer_guide_nd] |

Texere recognizes most of these words, and only in the role they play here: "since" as a reason, not "since 1990". Dismissive words and an unnamed "some argue" are left to you.

## 7. How to get there

1. **Understand the question.** State it precisely, in your own words [@roberts_7_nd; @rippon_brief_2008].
2. **Read slowly**, more than once, and reconstruct the argument: premises, conclusion, what supports each [@jones_how_nd; @cartaya_how_nd].
3. **Decide on a thesis** you can say in one short sentence [@millgram_writing_2024; @horban_writing_1993].
4. **Outline the argument** before the paper, with the objections and replies; the outline is most of the work [@pryor_guidelines_2012; @roberts_7_nd].
5. **Write the body first**, then the introduction and the conclusion [@anon_how_nd; @roberts_7_nd].
6. **Let it rest**, then revise: cut every paragraph the argument doesn't need, read it aloud, ask someone to read it. Expect at least two drafts [@anon_how_nd; @horban_writing_1993; @millgram_writing_2024; @pryor_guidelines_2012].
